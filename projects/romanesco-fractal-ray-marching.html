Romanesco Fractal Ray Marching

Overview

Raymarcher made following Michael Walczyk tutorial intro to ray marching.

Implicit Romanesco Broccoli surface developed using Rodrigo Setti's article on medium "The Broccoli Equation".

Program uses openGl to render a single triangle primitive covering the screen with texture coordinates normalized along screen space. Almost all of the magic is happening in the fragment shader, where signed distance funcitons are being used to create 3D worlds out of math. Shoutout Inigo Quilez, Michael Walczyk and many many more people who have documented their adventures in raymarching.

What is fractal raymarching?

Raymarching is a computer graphics technique where you slowly march a ray per fragment or pixel towards a scene defined by signed distance functions (SDFs). In fractal raymarching, the sdf we are comparing against recursivly adds detail on each layer defined by mathematical positioning for following layers. Many examples include renders people have made of fractal objects like the Menger Sponge. Inspired by these fractal raymarching projects and my favorite mathematical vegetable Romanesco Broccoli, I created this quick project to render a simple approximation of the form and grow my understanding of raymarching.

Why Romanesco Broccoli?

Romanesco Broccoli is a vegitable, more specifically a flower, that is very similar in texture and flavor to cauliflower. It is distinct for its fractal form, which follows a logarithmic spiral of self similar buds along the form of the flower. Its striking shape is the inspiration for this short project.

After debbugging for a while, I finally achieved the raymarched rendering of a cone with smaller cones following a logarithmic spiral. With some simple changes to the parameters of the spiral we can edit how it adds detail along the cone such as the number of children generated, the level of detail, the spacing, and the size of the buds.

A simple effect we can add to the render is a kind of ambient occlusion, where we accumulate more light the farther we travel. Based on how the raymarcher traverses through the screen, areas of crowded geometry will naturally have more steps to reach the innermost points of crevaces. This creates a render that looks far more real than a computer generated image, even if it is a quick approximation. Here is a render with just the ambient occlusion added on.
